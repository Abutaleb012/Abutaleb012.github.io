How I found oil spills hidden in hyperspectral imagery

Thin oil on water looks almost exactly like water. This is the story of the decisions behind my dissertation, what worked, what failed, and why I stopped trusting accuracy as a score.

Ali Abutaleb, PhD · 8 minute read

The same scene, the same detector family. Left: where the oil really is. Middle: a linear detector. Right: the kernel version with noise reduction. Oil in amber.

01The problem

A hyperspectral sensor records each pixel in hundreds of narrow bands, so every pixel carries a full reflectance spectrum. In theory that is enough to tell oil from water. In practice a thin sheen changes the spectrum only slightly, the signal sits close to the sensor noise, and the relationship between oil and background is not linear.

Classic detectors such as orthogonal subspace projection (OSP) and the matched subspace detector (MSD) assume a linear world. They draw a flat boundary between target and background. When the real boundary curves, they miss oil and flag clean water.

02The data

My main test scene was a hyperspectral image of an oil spill, cut to a region of interest of 436 × 449 pixels with 187 spectral bands. Every pixel in a ground truth image was labeled oil or not oil, which let me score each detector pixel by pixel.

5.6%of the scene is oil

That last number shaped everything that followed.

03Decision one: clean the signal first

Before detecting anything I ran a Minimum Noise Fraction (MNF) transform. MNF reorders the bands by signal-to-noise ratio, so the information that separates oil from water is concentrated and the noise is pushed aside. I kept a raw copy and an MNF copy of the scene and ran every detector on both, so I could measure what MNF was really worth.

04Decision two: let the kernel bend the boundary

The kernel trick maps each spectrum into a much higher-dimensional space where a flat boundary can separate classes that are tangled in the original space. The detector never computes that space directly. It only needs a kernel function that measures similarity between two spectra.

I tested three kernels: Gaussian RBF, polynomial, and spectral angle. I implemented four kernel detectors from the literature: KOSP, KMSD, KSMF, and KASD.

05Decision three: sample, don't swallow

A kernel detector builds a matrix that compares pixels with each other. For almost 200,000 pixels that matrix would hold tens of billions of entries, far beyond memory. So I used k-means clustering to pick 100 representative background spectra and 100 representative oil spectra, and built the kernel matrices from those. The detector then scores every pixel in the scene against those representatives.

06Decision four: stop trusting accuracy

Because only 5.6% of the scene is oil, a detector that says "no oil" everywhere is already 94% accurate. Linear OSP scored 0.90 accuracy, which sounds good, yet it found only 28% of the oil. Its F1 score, which balances false alarms against missed oil, was 0.35.

Rule I took away

For rare targets, report precision, recall, and F1. Accuracy hides failure.

07What happened

The RBF and polynomial kernels more than doubled the F1 score of linear OSP and lifted linear MSD by more than half. MNF helped the linear detectors and those two kernels. The best result came from the kernel matched subspace detector with MNF and a polynomial kernel, at an F1 score of 0.80.

08What surprised me

The spectral angle kernel, which sounds like the natural choice for spectra, was the weakest of the three kernels in every detector. With MNF, KMSD with a spectral angle kernel even fell from an F1 score of 0.70 to 0.25. My reading is that MNF reshapes the data in a way that an angle-based similarity does not handle well, while distance-based kernels such as RBF and polynomial benefit from it.

The other lesson was about honesty in reporting. The linear matched subspace detector reached 90% recall, a number that looks excellent on its own. Its precision was 0.34, meaning two of every three alarms were false. Reporting one metric alone would have told the wrong story either way.

09Where it led

The dissertation left me with a question I am still working on: how much of a detector's failure comes from the atmosphere rather than the detector? My current research tests five detectors on NASA EMIT satellite imagery as desert dust and coastal humidity build up. The answer so far: the spectral angle mapper (SAM) breaks down at an aerosol optical depth of 0.75, while MF, ACE, and CEM hold beyond 2.0.
